02 HISTORY OF AI

Explain key moments in the historical development of AI and ML and relate them to modern AI systems.

The idea of Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning can be dated all the way back to the beginnings of time. In the old stories of Greek Mythology, Talos was a giant automaton made of bronze. Other examples of ancient AI can be found in Jewish folklore and 1700s Slovakia.

The start of modern AI began with Alan Turing in the 1950s. Turing, often referred to as the father of AI, started the massive movement of modern AI with one question, “can machines think?” Meanwhile, a group of researchers gathered for a project during the summer of 1956 at Dartmouth University. This is where John McCarthy, one of the researchers at Dartmouth, came up with the term “Artificial Intelligence”. During the 1950s, AI was portrayed with symbols and simple logic. They used many “if statements” rather than learning and utilizing data.

AI continued to gain momentum during the ‘60s, with the release of ELIZA, which was a therapist chatbot that applied patterns to its logic. This made the chatbot seem like a real person, capable of understanding the user. However, by the 1970s, many presumed AI to be a “failed system”. The ‘70s was known as The First AI Winter. The Artificial Intelligence models in use did not scale to the complexities of the world, mainly because the hardware and software for this era was too limited in power.

Artificial Intelligence began to pick up momentum again in the 1980s as computing became mainstream. Now, instead of general intelligence, the AI models became experts in one specific domain. Some examples of models during this time were MYCIN and XCON. However, this ultimately fell short for another time in the late ‘80s through' 90s. This was known as The Second AI Winter. While AI systems were experts in their domain, they could not execute any functions outside of their given rulesets.

In the 1990s, hardware became stronger and more accessible, which brought about “thawing” from The Second AI Winter. In 1997, Deep Blue, which was a chess-playing super computer, defeated World Champion Garry Kasparov. This marked a major turning point in the evolution of Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning.

Through the 2000s, 2010s, and 2020s, data started to grow, and hardware and software became more advanced. With the Deep Learning Revolution in 2012, Transformers in 2017, and the release of ChatGPT in 2022, AI entered a generational boom.

With the simple AI models like ELIZA, to more complex one-domain experts like Deep Blue, to human-level intelligence models like ChatGPT, AI and Machine Learning has taken many steps throughout history to become what it is today. I believe that without the small steps and little improvements made to the earlier models, researchers would never had found out how to build complex systems like the ones we have today.

03 ETHICS OF AI

Identify and discuss ethical issues associated with AI and ML and their societal implications.

With the rise of Artificial Intelligence and the concept of Machine Learning in today’s society, many ethical issues are starting to form. Three important issues are algorithmic bias, calibrated trust, and dual use.

In the first case of algorithmic bias, a resume screener could potentially produce unequal outcomes if it learns from past hiring decisions that reflect discrimination. Although the system is not directly given the individual’s race or gender, it could rely on a proxy such as the individual’s name, school, or job history, that are connected to those characteristics. Ultimately, a person could be rejected from a job because of their race, gender, or other characteristics despite possessing all of the necessary qualifications and merit for that job. Automated hiring being implemented in society would disadvantage many people and could conceal discrimination, especially when employers treat the AI system’s decision as a priority.

In the second case of calibrated trust, the symptom checker raises the question of how much, and for what can society trust AI to make a decision on. The premise of the concern around the symptom checker depends on whether an individual can verify their own symptoms, how serious a wrong decision about their symptoms could be, and if a mistake could be reversible. In society, while a minor mistake can be easy to correct, a wrong answer could lead to major health consequences, and in some cases, even death. This is why AI should clearly state their limits on what their systems can make decisions on and people should be especially cautious when the stakes are high.

In the third case, voice cloning clearly shows the problem of dual use. The issue of dual use refers to when somebody creates an AI tool for a genuinely good purpose, but is misused to selfishly benefit the user. The responsibility of the wrongdoings are usually shared between the creator and the user. For example, the voice cloning tool could be used to help an individual communicate, but on the other hand, it could be used to imitate a person to scam or harass another individual. Some possible precautions of the voice cloning tool or any AI tool similar to it could include consent requirements and detection tools.

In conclusion, these three issues have significant impacts on society. They can influence access to jobs, affect healthcare, spread deception, and decrease overall trust in technology and AI. In all three cases, it can be seen that technology is always moving faster than the law and its regulators. That is why it is important to create safeguards to try and prevent as much harm as possible.
